The number is fuzzier than it looks

A US food label that says 500 calories can test at close to 600 and still pass the FDA's check, which treats calories as misbranded only when a composite lab sample comes in more than 20% over the label. Restaurant menus do fine on average, but when a lab tested 269 dishes from 42 restaurants, one in five came in at least 100 calories over the menu, and the lighter dishes were the ones most likely to run over.

Then you try to log it. USDA's own database lists 'one naan' at 262 calories in one entry (a 90 g, 3 oz piece) and 550 in another (a 10 inch, 177 g, 6 oz naan). A 2024 Australian study of apps sold in Australia found manual logging apps missed Asian meals by about 360 calories on average, and AI photo estimates were off too, with the authors flagging mixed dishes as the weak spot. A curry is a mixed dish. So a single number, stated to the calorie, is false precision. A range with its assumptions written out is the honest version, and that's what we're building.

What swings it

  • Sauce base: a curry with no cream or butter comes in at 107 kcal per 100 g in USDA's survey data, while a butter chicken made with cream, butter and yogurt lists 157 kcal per 100 g, about 47% more for the same spoonful.
  • Naan size and style: USDA lists one plain naan at 262 kcal (90 g piece) or 550 kcal (a 10 inch, 177 g naan), and the same US chain that lists a 379 kcal classic naan lists its stuffed Peshawari naan at 674 kcal.
  • Portion: lab-measured tikka masala meals in the same study averaged 548 g (19 oz) in San Francisco and 752 g (27 oz) in Boston, so the plate you get can be a third bigger depending on where you order.
  • Butter and oil finish: the chain's butter chicken carries 56 g of fat, more than half its calories, so a heavier or lighter hand with butter and cream moves the total more than anything else in the sauce.
  • How much sauce you mop up with the naan: the butter and cream are in the gravy, so how much of it you eat moves the total.

What we don't know

  • As of September 2026 we found no US chain that publishes butter chicken and naan together with gram weights. The best US menu source is a 2016 Tiffin Indian Cuisine PDF, calculated with food-service software rather than measured, and it gives no portion weights. The 1,275 kcal pairing is our sum of two separate rows.
  • We found no restaurant butter chicken in USDA FoodData Central. The closest entries are a generic 'Chicken curry' (no cream or butter in it) and a packaged supermarket butter chicken tray from Deep Foods, neither of which is a restaurant plate.
  • We found no US lab study that measured butter chicken itself. The closest measured dish is chicken tikka masala (Urban et al. 2016), reported as gross energy for the entree plus its accompanying sides, which reads somewhat higher than the calories your body actually absorbs. The paper does not say per meal whether those sides were rice, naan or both.
  • Sugar in the sauce is a wide open question: the chain lists 29 g for its butter chicken while the packaged tray works out to about 2 g, and we can't tell which is closer to a typical restaurant.

Worth knowing

  • Sodium. The chain's 2016 values for butter chicken plus classic naan come to about 2,440 mg of sodium, about 106% of the FDA Daily Value of 2,300 mg, in one dinner. Tiffin Indian Cuisine nutrition information (2016), FDA Daily Values
  • Saturated fat from cream and butter. The chain's 2016 values list 56 g of fat and 25 g of saturated fat in the butter chicken alone, 125% of the FDA Daily Value of 20 g before the naan, which lines up with the cream, butter and canola oil in USDA's listed butter chicken ingredients; that richness is most of what you are paying for. Tiffin Indian Cuisine nutrition information (2016)
  • Protein. The butter chicken carries 38 g of protein and the classic naan adds 14 g, so this plate has real protein, not just sauce and bread. Tiffin Indian Cuisine nutrition information (2016)
  • Sugar in the sauce. Restaurant butter chicken sauce can be sweet: the chain lists 29 g of sugar for the dish, versus about 2 g in a 255 g packaged tray, so sweetness varies a lot by kitchen. USDA FoodData Central, Deep Foods butter chicken (fdcId 2560191)

Verdict

Worth it, as the whole evening's meal rather than a side act, if it fits your day.

At around 1,250 calories you are getting a rich plate with a real hit of protein, and the cream and butter are exactly why it tastes the way it does. If you want it to go further, split the naan or leave some sauce in the bowl, but ordering it and enjoying it is the point. If you are watching sodium or saturated fat for a health reason, this plate is a lot of both.

Five food questions the loud voices fight about

Seed oils, diet soda, raw milk, fasting, whole wheat. For each one we lined up two well-known nutrition voices who disagree, then went to the published research to see who it backs and how firmly. Sometimes the answer is 'leans one way, not settled.' We say so. Where the research is uncertain, the card says uncertain.

Ten logged so far, from Layne Norton, Paul Saladino, Mark Hyman, Eric Berg, Gil Carvalho and others. Five below, with receipts.

Are seed oils (soybean, canola, safflower) harmful, or fine in place of saturated fat?

Seed oils (soybean, canola, corn, safflower)

Paul Saladino says Oct 2025 (upload date)

Says controlled human studies show that the excess linoleic acid from seed oils is harmful (more LDL oxidation, oxidative stress, inflammation, weaker mitochondrial function), and that people should limit polyunsaturated fats and put animal fats first.

Layne Norton says Apr 2025 (upload date)

Cites a 7-week randomized trial in which safflower oil (a seed oil that is mostly linoleic acid) lowered inflammation markers. He says linoleic acid does not raise arachidonic acid or prostaglandins, and he names Saladino as the source of the opposing claim.

What the research says

Confidence: moderate

On heart disease and death, the evidence leans toward Norton, but it is not settled. An AHA review of trials found that swapping saturated fat for polyunsaturated vegetable oil cut cardiovascular disease by about 30%. A 2025 study that followed 221,054 US adults for up to 33 years found that people who ate the most plant oil had 16% lower death rates, and those who ate the most butter had 15% higher death rates. That study is observational, so it shows a link and not proof of cause. On the other side, recovered data from the 1968-73 Minnesota Coronary Experiment showed that corn oil lowered cholesterol but did not lower deaths. The 2025-2030 US Dietary Guidelines say more research is needed on which fats are best. They name olive oil first, list butter or beef tallow as other options, and keep saturated fat under 10% of calories. None of these sources tests Saladino's specific claims about oxidation and inflammation.

  • Dietary Fats and Cardiovascular Disease: A Presidential Advisory From the American Heart Association (Sacks et al., Circulation 2017)
  • Butter and Plant-Based Oils Intake and Mortality (Zhang et al., JAMA Internal Medicine 2025)
  • Re-evaluation of the traditional diet-heart hypothesis: recovered data from the Minnesota Coronary Experiment (Ramsden et al., BMJ 2016)
  • Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2025-2030 (HHS/USDA, Jan 2026)

Do artificial sweeteners (diet soda, aspartame, sucralose) make you gain weight, or help you lose it?

Artificial (low-calorie) sweeteners and diet soda

Mark Hyman says Dec 2015

Says artificial sweeteners can be as bad as regular sugar or even worse, and tells readers to stop using them.

Layne Norton says Feb 2025 (upload date)

Says artificial sweeteners do not make people fat. His argument is that in randomized trials, people who replace sugar with sweeteners lose weight, and that the link seen in observational studies comes from poorer overall diet quality.

What the research says

Confidence: moderate

On weight, the trials back Norton. A review of 29 trials (2,267 people) found that people who swapped sugar for low-calorie sweeteners lost about 1 kg (2.2 lb) more than those who kept eating sugar. Compared with water, sweeteners made no difference to weight. Long-term safety is a separate question and it is not settled. The FDA says the sweeteners it has authorized are safe for the general population within the acceptable daily intake it sets for each one, and aspartame must carry a warning for people with PKU, who cannot process phenylalanine. The AHA (2018) said sweetened diet drinks may help adults who usually drink sugary ones, but it found little data on possible harms and advised against children drinking them for long periods. The 2025-2030 US Dietary Guidelines go further: they say no amount of non-nutritive sweeteners is recommended and advise limiting them. So current US guidance agrees with Hyman's advice to limit sweeteners, though advice to limit them is not a finding that they are toxic, and the trials do not support his claim that they are as bad as sugar for weight.

  • The effects of low-calorie sweeteners on energy intake and body weight: a systematic review and meta-analyses of sustained intervention studies (Rogers and Appleton, Int J Obes 2021)
  • Low-Calorie Sweetened Beverages and Cardiometabolic Health: A Science Advisory From the American Heart Association (Johnson et al., Circulation 2018)
  • FDA: Aspartame and Other Sweeteners in Food
  • 21 CFR 172.804, the aspartame label warning for people with PKU
  • Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2025-2030 (HHS/USDA, Jan 2026)

Raw (unpasteurized) milk: health food or food-safety risk?

Raw milk

Paul Saladino says Nov 2025 (upload date)

Says raw milk has been wrongly vilified, has many benefits for humans, and is, going by anecdote, easier to tolerate than pasteurized dairy.

Layne Norton says Nov 2025 (upload date)

Says raw milk is not a health food. In his view it has no proven health benefit and carries a much higher risk of foodborne illness, while pasteurization's main cost is a small loss of some vitamins.

What the research says

Confidence: strong

The evidence on risk is strong, and none of it shows an extra benefit from raw milk. The FDA says pasteurization kills the germs in raw milk without any significant impact on its nutritional quality. The FDA counts at least 133 US outbreaks linked to raw milk from 1987 to 2010, with 2,659 illnesses, 269 hospital stays and 3 deaths. US outbreak data from 1993 to 2006 show that, per amount consumed, unpasteurized dairy caused about 150 times more outbreaks than pasteurized dairy. Outbreaks rose from 30 in 2007-2009 to 51 in 2010-2012, and 81% of them involved milk bought in states where selling it was legal. None of these sources directly tests Saladino's claim that raw milk is easier to tolerate, which he himself calls anecdotal.

  • Raw Milk Misconceptions and the Danger of Raw Milk Consumption (FDA)
  • Nonpasteurized dairy products, disease outbreaks, and state laws, United States, 1993-2006 (Langer et al., Emerging Infectious Diseases 2012)
  • Increased outbreaks associated with nonpasteurized milk, United States, 2007-2012 (Mungai et al., Emerging Infectious Diseases 2015)

Is intermittent fasting a special tool for weight loss, or just another way to eat less?

Intermittent fasting (time-restricted eating, alternate-day fasting)

Eric Berg says Nov 2021 (upload date)

Calls intermittent fasting the most powerful tool for healthy weight loss. The video description also claims it raises growth hormone by 2000% and testosterone by 180%.

Gil Carvalho (Nutrition Made Simple!) says Feb 2022 (upload date)

Says that when calories are matched, intermittent fasting works no better than simply eating fewer calories, so it has no special fat-loss effect.

What the research says

Confidence: moderate

The trials back Carvalho. In a 12-month trial in Chicago, people on alternate-day fasting lost about the same weight as people on daily calorie cutting (6.0% vs 5.3%), and more of the fasting group dropped out (38% vs 29%). In a 12-week US trial, 116 adults eating in an 8-hour window lost no more weight than people eating three regular meals. The difference was 0.26 kg (about 0.6 lb) and was not significant. A 2025 review of 99 trials found that only alternate-day fasting beat plain calorie cutting, by about 1.3 kg (2.8 lb), and in trials that lasted 24 weeks or longer, no fasting method beat calorie cutting. So 'most powerful' is not supported. Fasting works about as well as other ways of eating less. None of these sources tests Berg's hormone numbers.

  • Effect of Alternate-Day Fasting on Weight Loss, Weight Maintenance, and Cardioprotection Among Metabolically Healthy Obese Adults (Trepanowski et al., JAMA Internal Medicine 2017)
  • Effects of Time-Restricted Eating on Weight Loss and Other Metabolic Parameters: The TREAT Randomized Clinical Trial (Lowe et al., JAMA Internal Medicine 2020)
  • Intermittent fasting strategies and their effects on body weight and other cardiometabolic risk factors: network meta-analysis (Semnani-Azad et al., BMJ 2025)

Are whole grains and whole wheat bread actually healthy?

Whole grains and whole wheat bread

Eric Berg says Dec 2022 (upload date)

Says 'healthy whole grains' are not really healthy. He claims whole wheat flour, like refined flour, has little fiber, vitamins, minerals or protein, and that the phytic acid in whole grains blocks mineral absorption.

Layne Norton says Jul 2022 (upload date)

Says eating whole grains is linked to lower cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes risk and does not raise inflammation. His video title calls it a debunk of Berg. It was posted in Jul 2022, before Berg's Dec 2022 video, so it answers an earlier Berg video.

What the research says

Confidence: moderate

The evidence leans toward Norton, but it is not settled. Long-term studies that follow people favor him: across 45 studies, each extra 90 g (about 3 oz) of whole grains a day went with 22% lower cardiovascular disease and 17% lower death from any cause. These studies are observational, so they show a link and not proof of cause. The trial evidence is thin, though. A Cochrane review found only 9 short trials (1,414 people, none longer than 16 weeks), none of which measured heart attacks or deaths, and the evidence on cholesterol was low quality. Thin trials do not make Berg's claims true. The 2025-2030 US Dietary Guidelines still say to prioritize fiber-rich whole grains (2 to 4 servings a day) and to cut white bread. These sources did not check Berg's claims about how much fiber and nutrients whole wheat flour contains.

  • Whole grain consumption and risk of cardiovascular disease, cancer, and all cause and cause specific mortality (Aune et al., BMJ 2016)
  • Whole grain cereals for the primary or secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease (Kelly et al., Cochrane 2017)
  • Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2025-2030 (HHS/USDA, Jan 2026)
